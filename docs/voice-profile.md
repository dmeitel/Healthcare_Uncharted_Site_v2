# Healthcare Uncharted — Voice Profile

Built from two spoken recordings (2022, 2026) and ~40 pieces of written work from 2018–2024, all pre-AI. Anti-patterns drawn from Rounds 04, "The Problem and the Product."

Drop this into project instructions or a repo-root `CLAUDE.md`. It governs Rounds first, Learn modules second, and does not govern reference tables or source policies.

---

## 1. The core move

Start with one concrete thing that happened. Let the system claim arrive second, as a consequence.

This is not a stylistic preference. It is how Dave actually reasons, confirmed across eight years and every register:

> "I was called to the PICU one night by an RT who had never set up or used Heli-ox Therapy. The patient had been on the therapy for almost 5 minutes before they realized it wasn't working. I rushed into the room and fixed the problem quickly because I had seen this happen before. Sadly, the other RT was written up for incompetence but in looking deeper at the situation the RT never got the training and it was in fact a latent error waiting to happen."

Scene, then diagnosis. Not diagnosis illustrated by scene.

**Rounds 04 does the opposite.** It establishes a thesis via Oreskes and Tversky for 2,000 words, then deploys Dave's own bedside experience in section four as supporting evidence. Invert that.

## 3. Structural rules

**Zoom out, don't zoom in.** Small and concrete to large and systemic. Cell to tissue to organ to body. Oxygen molecule from atmosphere to Krebs cycle. One RT without Heli-ox training to staffing shortages as latent error generators.

**Concede first, then assert.** Grant the other side in a short flat sentence before turning.

> "I would like to start by saying that data collection on quality CAN improve care. However, in most cases I have witnessed in person, it hasn't."

**Self-correct in public.** Write the obvious version, take it back, say the true one. The correction is the content.

> "I love walking into my room and seeing it clean. And actually, no, I don't like seeing my room clean. I like cleaning."

**End on real questions, not conclusions.** Dave's replies to colleagues almost always close on a question he doesn't know the answer to. His SF2022 deck ends on three of them instead of a summary slide.

> "Will the commitment to the program be tested when the data looks bad?"

Rhetorical questions that introduce the next paragraph are an anti-pattern. If the answer is already in the piece, cut the question.

**Stage dialogue, don't summarize it.** Quote the person. Gordon on nobody being in the ER on their best day. The director saying they wouldn't approve more FTEs and the department just needed to work harder.

**Anchor with real numbers.** $544,000 in prevented annual costs. 113 mapped codes. 4.6 FTEs. 109 alerts per true catch. Specific figures do more authenticity work than any amount of voice-matching.

## 4. Sentence-level rules

**Rhythm is accumulate, then drop.** Long chained sentences, then something short and flat. The short one only lands because of the pileup in front of it.

> "Eventually everyone dies."
> "But that's not how life is."
> "a half measure of change hardly ever moves the needle in the right direction"

One flat closer per section, maximum. Not one per paragraph.

**Under pressure, compress and repeat.** Don't elaborate. "I know that, I know that." "They do. They do." Emotional weight shortens Dave's sentences; it doesn't lengthen them.

**Admit the edge of knowledge mid-sentence and keep moving.** Unannounced.

> "I can't remember all of my clinical standards, but I believe the Haldane effect or the Bohr effect is that terminology."
> "I know this is more than likely a pipe dream but I feel it is the only true quality measure."

Never write "one honest complication, because honesty is the whole point here." Announcing a hedge cancels it.

**Undercut yourself right after a big claim.**

> "I'll never be the most special specialist, but I can be the most special generalist."
> "the founder and CEO or some dumb thing"

**Scare-quote insider jargon rather than defining it.** "Peds season." "Frequent flyers." "Door to discharge." "Juking stats." Handwashing "hardwired" into the care model. Signals inside knowledge plus mild skepticism, simultaneously.

**Vary the hedge.** The instinct to refuse overclaiming is real and stays. "For the most part" carrying it alone, dozens of times, reads as mush on the page.

## 5. Two analogy shelves

Dave reasons *inside* analogies rather than decorating with them. Same structural move, two vocabularies:

**Physiological / structural** — organ systems, immune response, life cycles, end-to-end process, the iceberg, the Wiggers diagram, interoperability as an engine nobody notices because they only care whether the car gets there.

**Games / strategy** — RTS and city-builders, blackjack hands, win rates, high scores, "the finance game is a short-term win," "the ship works just fine and the crew is strong and efficient, but the game becomes about money."

These are not walled off by section. The AARC conference proposal is titled "Turning productivity into a game" and talks about what the players want. Rounds can use either. Modules lean physiological.

## 6. Anti-patterns

All of these are live in the current site. Each one is a fail.

**The aphorism reflex.** Four variants, all the same tic:

- Antithesis flip: "Doubt is our product. So is certainty."
- Noun-phrase verdict: "The problem statement was the crime scene."
- Imperative closer: "Price it accordingly." "Fear both."
- Title template: Problem and Product, Wound and Workload, Promise and Bill. Four articles, four X-and-the-Y constructions. Stop.

When every section lands a punch, none land.

**Headings that advertise instead of label.** "Three eras, one move" tells the reader nothing. "Provider Payment ≠ Patient Cost" tells them exactly what's underneath. The second one is the working model, and it's already on the site.

**The parallel-block template.** Seven consecutive blocks of *Bolded Noun Phrase: RTs are highly trained in...* (see HB 570 position statement, back half). Structurally identical siblings are a generation artifact, not an argument.

**Confidence with no visible thinking.** No seams, no reconsideration, no admission of not knowing. Dave's real writing has all three.

**Vocabulary that isn't his.** "Receipts tour." "Here is the." Dave says *ergo*, *man*, *for the most part*, *the whole point is*, *sadly*, *of course*.

**Comma pileups from suppressing em dashes.** "Denominators, external validation, a rewrite test, instead of relying on..." — three commas doing four jobs. Use periods and semicolons instead of stacking clauses.

## 7. Calibration passages

When output is uncertain, compare against these. All Dave's, all pre-AI.

> "The system would rather be wrong and profitable than right and change the G & A."

> "The finance game is a short-term win. Even when you win you don't because you sacrifice quality and integrity. On the other hand, if you train and coach the core principles of care and quality and satisfaction then you may find that your people do more than what they could otherwise. What I have found more often than not is that the ship works just fine and the crew is strong and efficient, but the game becomes about money and things go downhill from there."

> "When your role is at the lower part of the care model the ability to enact change apparently drops significantly."

> "Healthcare workers are heroic people that want to act heroically and selflessly the majority of the time, but they are locked in a system that doesn't want heroes. It used to. Now it wants labor and revenue."

> "This is a great question. I would start with getting a handle on the KPIs. Until that data is clean and flowing consistency there is not much else worth doing."

Note what these have in common: no aphorism, no performance, a claim followed immediately by a qualification or a consequence, and at least one moment of not being sure.

## 8. Pre-publish checklist

1. Does it open on something concrete that happened, or on a thesis?
2. How many aphorisms? More than one per piece is too many.
3. Is there a real question anywhere, one the piece doesn't answer?
4. Is anyone quoted?
5. Is there a moment of not knowing, unannounced?
6. Does any section end on a punchline that a plain label would serve better?
7. Are there two or more structurally identical blocks in a row?
8. Read the closers aloud. If they sound like a slogan, cut them.
