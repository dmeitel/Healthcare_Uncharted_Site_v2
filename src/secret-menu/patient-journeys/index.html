Patient Populations: The Twenty States Healthcare Serves

Healthcare sorts patients two ways out of habit: by diagnosis, and by the department they happen to be standing in. Neither says much about what the person needs. This module sorts them a third way, by the state they are in, across three journeys that run alongside each other.

Part 1

Why state, not diagnosis

A diagnosis describes what is wrong. A department describes where the person is standing. Neither one tells you what the system has to do next, which is why two patients with the same diagnosis can need completely different things from it.

State does tell you. A person with diabetes who is well controlled and a person with diabetes in ketoacidosis share a diagnosis and share almost nothing else: not the setting, not the staffing, not the cost, not the time horizon. The first is a clinic relationship measured in years. The second is an ICU bed measured in hours.

The twenty states below are the ones this site uses. They are not a clinical taxonomy and they are not exhaustive. They are the smallest set that still lets you ask a useful question: who is the system serving right now, and what does that cost.

A person occupies more than one of these at a time and moves between them, usually more than once. A pregnancy is its own journey running beside the acuity one, and a delivery hands off to a third.

Part 5

How to read the tiers

Tier is the only ordering these states carry, and it runs in the direction acuity runs. Tier 0 is a person the system has barely met. Tier 1 is a person it is actively working with. Tier 2 is a person it is spending heavily to hold.

The tiers are worth reading as a cost curve as much as a clinical one. The cheapest states to serve are the ones with the least attention on them, and the most expensive states are reached by moving through the cheap ones without anyone noticing. That is the argument for prevention stated as a fact about where people end up, rather than as a slogan.

Two of the twenty have no cure as a goal at all. Palliative and neonatal palliative sit at tier 2 because the system is still spending, and still working hard, at a point where the thing it is working toward has changed.

Part 7

Where this page came from, and how it is built

These twenty states were not written for an article. They were written for the career tree, which has a second class beside Roles called Populations: the same board, but arranged around who you care for instead of what your title is. The switch that would have let a visitor see it has been set to hidden at every screen width since it was built, so the nodes have shipped on every page load and nobody has ever reached one.

That raised a question with two obvious answers, delete them or wire the switch back up. On 2026-09-21 David took a third: keep the content and give it a reader, because the material is a reasonable place for someone to start if they have never thought about healthcare in terms of who it serves rather than who works in it.

The page reads the nodes straight out of src/assets/data/career-tree.json at build time, through src/_data/patientJourneys.js. Nothing is copied. That matters more than it sounds: a copy would let the article and the tool drift apart silently, and this way the build fails loudly if the twenty ever become nineteen. The data file counts them and refuses to build a set it does not recognise.

Draft, and in the Secret Menu on purpose. The writing has not had a voice pass, the twenty states have not been checked against a source, and the career tree's hidden switch is still hidden. None of that is settled.
