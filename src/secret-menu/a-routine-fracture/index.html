Tracing a Broken Arm Through the Healthcare System

A week under the waterline
TL;DR

Dave Doe broke an arm playing flag football on a Thursday night. Over the next eight days the elbow was put back together. Getting there took two EDs, a pharmacy, an orthopedic clinic, a surgery center and a primary care office, and far more coordination than Dave could see as the patient.

  • Five registrations at five places, four imaging studies of the same elbow before surgery, three radiologists, two sedations, and three separate EHRs.
  • The trauma center's plan was an orthopedic visit early the next week and surgery after that, a timeline within the usual range once a fracture is reduced and splinted. Dave called four or five clinics directly and was seen Monday. Surgery was on day five.
  • The one large payment so far was $1,840.52 at the surgery center, an estimate toward its own facility bill; the surgeon and anesthesia bill separately. Apart from the pharmacy fills, which settle at the counter, nothing has come back from the insurer yet.
POSTED Draft System Layers map READ 15 min

At about 6:30 that Thursday evening, Dave landed on the left elbow at the park and got up holding an arm that looked wrong. Eight days later Dave sat in a primary care office, doing fine.

Dave Doe is a stand-in name for the author. Dave worked as a respiratory therapist in an ICU for years before moving into informatics, and now manages a clinical informatics team. That job means knowing what a registration sets in motion downstream, and what an order does once it is signed. Being the patient, with one arm in a splint, was new. So Dave documented the week and mapped it. It kept Dave's mind busy through the pain and the days of rest.

Purpose

Why this case

Most of Dave's day job is evaluating healthcare processes: how work moves through a system, and where it stalls. Informatics teams test that work constantly. They build test patients and walk new workflows through quality assurance before a real patient ever touches them. None of those test patients has ever been in pain, or had to find a surgeon on a Friday.

That week Dave could trace an order to the image it produced and on to the decision it drove, which led to another registration somewhere else.

Even in the bedside years, Dave rarely thought about how all of these systems fit together, and had very little exposure to how other specialties, like orthopedics or cardiology, run their own parts of it. This week put several of those parts in view at once.

A patient moves through healthcare the way a customer moves through a restaurant, or at least that is how it feels from the table. In healthcare, though, you agree to pay later for a meal nobody has chosen yet, and partway through you may be sent to a different kitchen. What happens around the patient is far more complicated than the dining room suggests, and a lot of it depends on the systems each organization happens to use.

Most of the care happened inside a single health system. Much of it matched workflows Dave knows from work; the rest belonged to a separate organization or sat outside a respiratory therapist's training, and Dave could only infer those parts.

The Week

The week is presented below in three views. Start with the story, the patient's view: what Dave went through, one step at a time, with pain and anxiety on a rough 0 to 10 scale. Each step set off more than showed at the bedside, and the dots on each card count it. Select the dots to open a single step, or use the buttons that stay at the top of the page to show a layer across the whole week. The last layer, Questions to ask, lists what Dave would suggest any patient in the same position ask at that point.

The paper trail is the technical view. It follows the records, from where each one started to the last place it reached. The whole map places every layer side by side. Anywhere you see a dashed outline, the step probably happened but Dave's own records cannot confirm it, or it has not happened yet.

    The trauma center

      Dave's pain hit a 10 in the first ED before the first sedation, and it was back at a 10 when the transfer was decided. Anxiety stayed low until then, because the plan was still to fix it in that building.

      Friday to Monday

      Finding a surgeon

        Calling around got Dave seen sooner. Whether it changed anything clinically, Dave does not know: a clinic visit early the next week and surgery soon after is within the usual range for a fracture that has been reduced and splinted, and if the ED placed a referral order, a scheduler may have called before Tuesday. Dave did not wait long enough to find out. The records also do not show whether the joint stayed in place in the splint over those four days. How many patients go home unsure whether they are supposed to call or wait to be called?

        Tuesday

        Surgery day

          Anxiety climbed to a 7 when the transfer was decided and an 8 on arrival at the trauma center. Those were handoffs, points where Dave did not know who was responsible for the care next. It fell to a 3 after the Monday visit and jumped back to an 8 at check-in, mostly over the $1,840.52 payment. The 9 in pre-op was ordinary nerves before surgery.

          Medicare's conditions for surgery centers require that patients be discharged in the company of a responsible adult, unless the attending physician exempts them. Dave's care partner appears in three records all week: the emergency contact taken at the first registration, the clinic note that says he came along and, presumably, the surgery center's discharge. Everything else he did, from the driving to serving as a second set of ears during the consent process, happened outside every system Dave can see.

          The surgery center is a separately certified facility. It runs its own pre-surgical assessment on admission and uses its own consents, on an EHR separate from the health system's. Medicare's rules let a surgery center decide which patients need a history and physical before surgery. For a plating under anesthesia, Dave's H&P almost certainly had to be in the record, and the surgery went ahead, so it probably was. It may have come by fax, by an upload from the surgeon's office or through access to the health system's chart. The surgeon may also have written it there that day, in which case nothing was carried over at all, and Dave's records do not say which.

                Follow a record
                Records

                Where the records stopped

                Within the health system, the chart did not have to move. The first ED, the trauma center and the clinic all chart in one EHR. As far as Dave can tell, they keep images in one archive too: the trauma center's CT was read against "the previous study," and the surgeon had reviewed all four studies before the clinic visit. The gaps were at the edges, at the two places on separate EHRs, plus one inside: the portal shows no images. The pharmacy runs its own system too. E-prescriptions travel between systems on a standard route, so the prescription got there and nobody had to think about it.

                Medicare's hospital rules require hospitals with qualifying electronic systems to make a reasonable effort to notify a patient's established primary care practitioner when the patient registers in the ED and again at discharge or transfer, directly or through a health information exchange. That is the likely origin of the message Dave's PCP received. It may have gone through a health information exchange, or as a secure message or fax.

                Leaving the images out of the portal does not limit Dave's right to them: under HIPAA a patient can request copies of their images, and the provider has 30 days to act on the request, with one 30-day extension if it explains the delay in writing.

                Repetition

                What happened more than once

                Some of that repetition is medicine working as intended. The first reduction did not get the radius fully back in place, so a second reduction and a second CT were the right call. More than one clinician said Dave had been "very successful" in breaking that arm, which Dave has chosen to take as a compliment.

                Some of it is structural. Each visit is billed on its own, so each one confirms coverage and opens its own account, even though the insurance never changed. Inside the health system, those were new encounters on one chart. Reading the chart with an informaticist's eye, Dave can sort most of the repeats into medicine and structure. Dave is not sure a patient without that background could.

                Every front desk also asked for Dave's name and birth date, the same two identifiers printed on every wristband. That is how registration finds the existing chart and avoids starting a duplicate. With no shared patient number, it is also how a system on another EHR would match Dave to the first one's records.

                Cost

                What it cost, so far

                The cost surfaced above the waterline once, as a payment. At check-in on surgery day Dave paid $1,840.52 to the surgery center, which was in network, even though the out-of-pocket maximum for the year seemed met already. Dave had spent close to it earlier in the plan year, and neither ED nor the Monday clinic had collected a copay at the desk. Plenty of EDs and clinics skip the copay at the desk and bill it once the claim processes; Dave's PCP skipped it too, three days later. A copay nobody collects at the desk and an out-of-pocket maximum that has been met are different things, and only the second means the plan pays 100% of covered in-network costs for the rest of the plan year.

                The surgery center's number most likely came from an eligibility check against the insurer's running totals. Those totals move only as claims process, and five days after the injury the ED claims probably had not. Whatever order the claims process in, the maximum caps what Dave pays in network for the year; the order mostly decides which place collects it. If the ED claims reach the maximum first, the surgery center refunds and the ED bills carry more. If the surgery center's estimate simply ran high, it refunds the difference.

                Counting the way these places usually bill, with separate facility, physician, radiologist, surgeon and anesthesia claims, Dave expects a dozen or more claims from this week. Was the $1,840.52 owed, and to whom? Dave does not know yet. No explanation of benefits has arrived, which is not unusual eight days out, and this piece gets a dated update when they do.

                Day 8

                Where Dave is now

                Dave sees the surgeon again on Thursday. Eight days out, the pain is about a 1 with pain medicine as needed, and the anxiety has mostly resolved. For the most part, the healthcare system is designed to help people, and that week it did: everyone Dave met was skilled, and the elbow is back together.

                Scale

                How common this is

                In 2022, US emergency departments handled an estimated 39.6 million visits for injuries, about a quarter of all ED visits and roughly 108,000 a day, according to the CDC's National Hospital Ambulatory Medical Care Survey. About 482,000 of those visits listed a forearm fracture as the primary diagnosis, roughly 1,300 a day. Most of those are breaks near the wrist; a review of the research puts wrist-end fractures at as many as three in four forearm fractures. An elbow fracture-dislocation that needs a transfer and a plate is a small share.

                Surgery is harder to count, because much of it happens outside hospitals. In 2023, hospital-owned outpatient facilities alone handled about 485,000 surgical visits whose main procedure treated a fracture or dislocation, around 1,330 a day, and about 129,000 of them involved the radius and ulna, according to AHRQ's Nationwide Ambulatory Surgery Sample. That count leaves out independent surgery centers, which may include the one Dave used. In the most recent federal survey that counted them, from 2010, nearly half of all ambulatory procedures took place in surgery centers.

                By those numbers the path Dave's week took was routine, even though the injury sat at the severe end. More than a thousand people a day start some version of it, and the system handles each of them with the same registrations, orders, images and handoffs counted here. This was the first time Dave watched it from the bed.
