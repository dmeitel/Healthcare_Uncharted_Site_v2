Method AI and standard work

AI and standard work.

A guide for healthcare leaders and operations staff on bringing AI into routine work. It starts from a workflow you already know, adds what AI needs one level at a time, and ends with a planning card for one task of your own.

Clinical Informatics CDSS & AI Tools
Before the levels

A Monday task

Take a unit manager who builds the same page every Monday before the 0700 staffing huddle. The weekend's call-offs come from the scheduling system and the census from another screen; then the week's open shifts, the float pool's list, and a note or two from the night charge nurse. Most of the forty minutes goes to copying numbers from one screen into another, and the page has looked the same for three years.

That page is standard work, even if nobody on the unit calls it that. It has a job (the huddle needs a staffing picture), a set of steps, and a known way of going wrong: the call-off that came in at 0500, after the page was built. It is also the kind of task people now hand to AI, sometimes well and often badly. What decides which is whether the person handing it over could write the steps down.

AI works best on a workflow the team already understands. Added to one nobody understands, it repeats that workflow's mistakes faster. So the first level is the work itself, before any tool, and each level after it adds what AI needs to a task the team could already do by hand.

Why it works this way

What a prompt and a workflow share

Prompt engineering is the name for writing the instructions an AI model works from, and the name makes it sound like a technical specialty. Read a prompt that works well, though, and most of it would be at home in a policy or a new hire's training. It says what the work is for, what to read and what good looks like; it gives the steps and what to do when something is unusual; and it says what to hand over at the end.

That list is a workflow. Process improvement already has a name for each part of it, and AI work has a second name for most of them.

The overlap is the reason people who write good standard work tend to write good prompts on their first try. Most of the prompt already exists in their procedure. A team that has never written its work down hands the tool nothing to follow, whatever the tool costs.

It also explains the most common failure. People type a request the way they would ask a colleague, and they leave out what the colleague would already know: which unit, which template, which deadline, who reads the result. A colleague fills those gaps from experience. A model fills them with whatever sounds most likely.

Agents add the parts of a workflow that happen between people. An agent can be given tools to collect its own inputs, it can take several steps in a row, and it can pass work to the next role. Each of those is a workflow decision before it is a technical one: who may touch which system, where the work stops for a person, and what gets written down along the way. Informatics teams make those decisions about every build already, and an agent is one more thing to build that way.

Rules and AI

Where a rule is enough

A flowchart is the usual way to draw a process, and it suits work that follows rules. Every case is listed ahead of time and each one has a path. Software built from rules does the same thing every time, which is its strength, and it has no answer for a case nobody listed.

A language model earns its place on exactly those cases: the request written in a hurry, the note that mixes three problems in one paragraph, the policy question phrased a way nobody has phrased it before. The same flexibility means it can answer one request two ways on two different days. Work built on a model has to expect variation and catch it, every time.

So AI work gets written down the way standard work does: the inputs it reads, the instructions, a check on every result before it is used, and a review that changes the instructions when the check keeps catching the same problem. A written standard also moves well. The unit down the hall can use the same instructions with its own reports and templates swapped in.

Rules still win where the work never varies. A lookup against a fixed table, or a calculation with every number known, is safer and cheaper as a plain rule, and a good plan says which of its steps are rules.

The four levels

What each level adds

Each level keeps everything from the one before it and adds its own parts. A team can stop at any level, and plenty of tasks never need the last two.

Level 1

Know the workflow

Standard work is the current best-known way to do a routine task, written down so that anyone on the team who follows it gets the same result. The idea comes from Lean, and AHRQ's Patient Safety Network carries it into health care in those terms. It is never finished. When someone finds a better way, the standard changes and everyone moves to it. Process Engineering covers how to map a workflow and improve it; this level asks only whether you can describe one.

Four answers come first, and none of them involve AI. The first is the job and who it is for. The huddle page exists so the charge nurse can move staff before 0730, which makes the charge nurse its reader and 0730 its deadline.

Second, what done looks like, written as a check someone else could run. "Every open shift for the next seven days, with the float pool names beside each" can be checked against the schedule. "A good staffing picture" cannot be checked by anyone but the person who wrote it.

Then the steps as they happen now, workarounds included. If the census comes from a report that refreshes at 0600, that refresh belongs in the steps, because a page built at 0550 carries yesterday's numbers. Last, where the task goes wrong today. Every routine task has its known failures, and they are the first thing a later level has to guard against.

Then count the task as it runs now, before any tool touches it. For two weeks, write down how long it takes and each time it goes wrong; on the Monday page that might be forty minutes a week and one late call-off missed. Those numbers are the baseline. Without them nobody can say later whether AI made the task better, and the section on measuring, after level 3, is built on them.

A useful test before going further: hand the written steps to a new hire. If they cannot build the page from them, an AI tool will struggle too, and it will produce something that looks finished anyway.

The planning card at the end of this module starts with these four answers and the baseline. Filling them in for one of your own tasks takes about ten minutes, and the counting takes the two weeks.

Level 2

AI on your own task

Most people start here, one person using a general AI assistant on their own task. AI is already in the room: in the American Medical Association's survey of early 2026, 72 percent of physicians said they use at least one of 17 named kinds of AI in their practice, most often to summarize medical research. At this level the person controls everything that goes in, and five parts matter more than the choice of tool.

The tool knows nothing about your unit. What it reads is all it has, so the context is the staffing policy, last Monday's page, and the template the charge nurses expect. Given those, it works from your facts. Given nothing, it fills the gaps with facts that sound right, which NIST's profile for generative AI calls confabulation: content stated with confidence that turns out to be false.

Context has a second half, which is what stays out. Patient information goes only into tools your organization has approved for it, and a general assistant someone signed up for at home is not one of them. Ask your informatics or privacy team which tools are approved before the first patient name goes anywhere. The Joint Commission's 2026 review guide for its AI certification looks for exactly that rule, a policy that keeps protected health information out of public AI tools.

An example of good work does more than a page of instructions. One staffing page the charge nurses liked, or ten requests a senior analyst classified with the reason for each, shows the tool the standard in a form it can copy. When the output feels generic, this is usually what is missing.

Ask it to keep what it found apart from what it worked out. A number copied from the call-off list can be checked against the call-off list. A number the tool inferred, or filled in because the list was a row short, needs a person's judgment, and on the page the two look the same unless the instructions ask for them to be marked. This is the everyday guard against confabulation: the tool says when it is guessing, and a missing number is written as missing.

Then check it before using it. The check you wrote at level 1 becomes the gate: compare the draft against its sources before it reaches the huddle. A draft that is right nine Mondays out of ten is wrong on the tenth, and that tenth error lands on a unit that is already short a nurse.

Put together for the Monday page, those parts make a prompt like this one. The numbers match the key beside it.

Each missing part fails in its own way, which turns out to be useful. Output that could have been written for any hospital usually means there was no example. Numbers that appear in no source mean the tool had no context and filled the space. An error that reaches the huddle is a missing check.

In informatics the same level looks like an analyst pasting a new request into an assistant along with the routing standard and a handful of classified examples, then asking for a draft class: break / fix, net new, enhancement, or optimization. The analyst still makes the call. The draft saves the reading, and the classes come from Informatics Work: Routing Requests.

Using AI to think a problem through

Everything above uses the assistant to make something: a page, a summary, a draft class. The same assistant can be used earlier, before anything is made, to think a problem through. Given a plain description of the problem, it can say the problem back in other words, which shows whether you described it well. It can list what you seem to be assuming, argue for the option you were about to drop, and name what would have to be true for your plan to fail.

Used this way, the answer is a set of arguments for a person to weigh, and the decision stays with that person. Ask for the case against your plan along with the case for it, since an answer that only agrees with you adds nothing you did not already have. NIST's generative AI profile lists over-reliance among the risks of how people and AI systems work together, and it applies to an argument as much as to a draft: one that sounds right still has to be held up against what you know about the unit.

Level 3

Agents in a team workflow

An agent is software that takes steps toward a goal someone set. It reads, looks things up, writes drafts, and uses other systems through tools. At level 2 a person stood between every step and the result. At level 3 the agent works through several steps on its own before anyone looks, so the plan needs parts that say what it may do and where it has to stop.

Start with what it may touch. Reading the request queue is one permission. Assigning a ticket is another, and sending the requester a message is a third, and each one gets granted on purpose. The safest agent reads widely and writes only drafts, and most teams should stay there until the record says otherwise.

Where it stops is written down too. Some cases always go to a person: anything the agent cannot place with confidence, anything marked urgent, anything that touches a clinical workflow. A good stop list is short and specific, and it grows when the record shows a case the agent should have handed over.

Sign-off belongs to a named role, such as the analyst on queue duty, so it is clear who owns a mistake. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework asks for the same thing in its own terms: human oversight that is defined, with roles kept distinct. And the agent keeps a record: each draft, the reason it gave, and whether the person kept it or changed it. That record is how the instructions improve, through the same Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle a team already runs on its own work, the testing loop of IHI's Model for Improvement.

The check and the sign-off are never optional at this level. Leave either out, and the agent's mistakes reach production without anyone seeing them first.

Running, the request queue plan is a loop, and a person closes it on every request.

A prior authorization packet shows the pattern outside informatics. An agent gathers what the payer's form asks for from the chart and drafts the packet; a person reviews it and submits it. The volume is why it matters: in an AMA survey fielded in December 2025, physicians who handle prior authorizations reported an average of 40 a week, taking about 13 hours of physician and staff time.

Written out with all three levels filled in, the request queue looks like the card below. It is the same planning card that waits at the end of this module, completed for one informatics task.

The task
Read each new request in the informatics queue, give it a class and an owning team, and ask the requester for anything missing.

The requester and the team that will build it. Done: every request has a class, an owner and a next step within one business day, and the requester knows what happens next.

How long it takes now

Level 2 · AI on your own task

What it reads
The routing standard, the team list, and the last month of classified requests.
What it is never given
Patient identifiers a requester pasted in by mistake; the request is redacted before it is read.
An example of good work
Ten requests a senior analyst classified, each with the reason.
Facts apart from guesses
The draft quotes the words in the request that decided its class, and says so when it is guessing.
The check before it is used
The analyst compares the draft class against the three tests before anything is assigned.

Level 3 · Agents in a team workflow

What it may touch
It reads the queue and writes a draft class and a draft question for the requester. It may not assign, close, or message anyone.
Where it stops
Anything it cannot class with confidence, anything marked urgent, and anything that changes a clinical workflow go straight to the analyst.
Who signs off
The analyst on queue duty.
What it records
Its draft, its reason, and whether the analyst kept or changed it, reviewed by the team each week.

Some work stays with people whatever the level. Clinical decisions do. So does anything that cannot be undone, like sending, deleting or ordering, and an agent should never see patient information its task does not need. Deciding which AI tasks a department allows in the first place is a governance question, and Leading the AI Transition takes it up.

Before level 4

Measuring whether it works

A task can feel faster with AI and take longer, because the time saved on the draft moves into the check. The only way to tell is to compare it with the task as it ran by hand, which is why level 1 ends with a baseline. IHI's Model for Improvement makes this one of its three questions: how will we know that a change is an improvement?

The test itself is a pilot: one task, one team, a few weeks, and every result checked before it is used. It is the small-scale test of the Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle, and it is cheap to stop if it goes badly. The check produces most of the numbers on its own, since each time a person keeps, changes or throws out a draft, that is a count.

Corrections are usually the first number to move. When they fall over the pilot, the instructions and the example are doing their job. Flat corrections usually mean a part of the plan is missing, most often the example or the context. A count that never falls says the task may be better as a plain rule, or may not suit AI.

Time needs a second look. IHI calls a measure that watches for new problems in another part of a system a balancing measure, and here the check is that measure. A draft that saves thirty minutes and takes twenty-five to check has saved five. If people start skipping the check to keep those minutes, the errors that get through will show it a few weeks later.

Kept through the pilot and after it, these counts are the evidence that level 4 asks each AI task to carry.

Level 4

AI across a department

Once one team's AI-supported workflow works, other teams ask for it. A department then needs what a pharmacy already keeps for medications: a list of every AI task in use, an owner for each, the evidence that it works, and a date to look at it again. Leading the AI Transition makes the case for running it like a formulary. Public guidance points the same way. In September 2025 the Joint Commission and the Coalition for Health AI asked health systems for AI governance, ongoing monitoring, and training for the people who use the tools. NIST's framework sorts the same work into four functions: govern, map, measure and manage. Building that list one department at a time is the subject of the modules that follow this one.

How many AI tasks a department can run before the list itself needs a person to keep it is an open question, and probably a different number for every department.
