Alarm fatigue: what the evidence says.

The numbers behind the Alarm Fatigue game: how many alarms a monitored bed produces, how many of them mean anything, what happened when nobody answered, and what The Joint Commission required hospitals to do about it.

By David Eitel ~4 min · five sections Four primary sources, checked 2026-09-20

The mechanism

The definition

Alarm fatigue is what happens to a clinician who hears too many alarm signals, most of which require nothing. The Joint Commission's 2013 Sentinel Event Alert on medical device alarms describes the mechanism in three parts: devices whose alarms sound alike, default settings that are never adjusted for the patient, and a failure to respond to the signals that do matter. Repeated exposure to signals that carry no clinical meaning makes the clinician less responsive to all of them, including the one that does.

The devices involved are the standard equipment of a monitored bed: the bedside physiologic monitor, telemetry, pulse oximetry, blood pressure cuffs, infusion pumps, ventilators, and the central station that aggregates them. Each runs its own alarm logic on its own defaults, and none of it is coordinated with the device next to it.

Source: The Joint Commission, Sentinel Event Alert issue 50, Medical device alarm safety in hospitals, April 2013. Checked 2026-09-20.
The count

How many alarms a bed produces

The Sentinel Event Alert puts the count at several hundred alarm signals per patient per day, depending on the unit, and estimates that between 85 and 99 percent of them do not require clinical intervention. The causes it names are alarm conditions set too tight, defaults not adjusted for the patient or the population, dried-out ECG electrodes, and sensors out of position.

The largest single measurement comes from the University of California, San Francisco. In March 2013, Drew and colleagues logged every alarm from every bed in five adult intensive care units for 31 days: 461 patients, 2,558,760 unique alarms. Of those, 381,560 were audible, an average of 187 audible alarms per bed per day. The team then annotated 12,671 arrhythmia alarms by hand and found 88.8 percent were false positives. The team traced the false positives to inappropriate alarm settings, persistent atrial fibrillation, events such as premature ventricular contractions and brief ST-segment spikes that need no action, low-amplitude QRS complexes that fooled the counting, and wide QRS complexes from bundle branch block or a paced rhythm.

The harm

What happened when nobody answered

Between January 2009 and June 2012, 98 alarm-related events were reported to The Joint Commission's Sentinel Event database. Of the 98, 80 resulted in death. Thirteen left permanent loss of function, and five led to unexpected additional care or a longer stay. Ninety-four of the events occurred in hospitals, most of them in telemetry, intensive care, general medicine, and emergency department areas. The injuries and deaths were traced back through falls, delays in treatment, ventilator use, and medication errors, and the alert states plainly that alarm-related events are underreported.

Reporting to the Sentinel Event database is voluntary, so the 98 events understate the total.

Source: Sentinel Event Alert 50, April 2013 (indexed as Sentinel Event Alert 2013;(50):1-3, PMID 23767076). Checked 2026-09-20.
The rule

What the regulators required

ECRI Institute has reported on alarm hazards since 2007. The Sentinel Event Alert notes that on ECRI's annual Top 10 Health Technology Hazards list, clinical alarm conditions consistently appeared as the first or second most critical hazard. In the 2015 edition the number one hazard was alarm hazards, described as inadequate alarm configuration policies and practices.

In July 2013 The Joint Commission announced a new National Patient Safety Goal, NPSG.06.01.01, on clinical alarm safety for hospitals and critical access hospitals. It has four elements of performance, phased in over two years.

  • Leadership establishes alarm system safety as an organizational priority, from January 2014.
  • During 2014, the organization identifies the alarm signals most important to manage, using its own incident history, published guidance, and clinical input.
  • From January 2016, written policies and procedures govern those alarms: clinically appropriate settings, when a signal can be disabled, when parameters can be changed, who has the authority to set, change, or turn them off, and how the organization monitors and responds.
  • Staff and licensed practitioners are educated on the purpose and operation of the alarm systems they are responsible for, also from January 2016.
Sources: Sentinel Event Alert 50, April 2013; The Joint Commission, Joint Commission Perspectives, vol. 33 no. 7, July 2013, announcing NPSG.06.01.01 (copy hosted by America's Essential Hospitals); ECRI Institute, Top 10 Health Technology Hazards for 2015. Checked 2026-09-20.

The model

What the game models

The Alarm Fatigue game compresses the evidence above into a twelve-hour shift. Each upgrade adds a device with its own alarm, so the several-hundred-a-day figure is built one purchase at a time. Alarms that carry nothing are the bulk of the volume, and the ones that matter sound like the rest, which is the Sentinel Event Alert's first contributing factor. The game's audio wears down the more a player silences it, a mechanical stand-in for desensitization.

The game leaves out most of what the National Patient Safety Goal is about. It has no default settings to adjust, no policy on who may change a parameter, no electrode maintenance, and no staffing model beyond the techs a player hires. The regulators' answer to alarm fatigue is organizational, not individual; the game shows the individual's side of the problem, which is the part the evidence measures in alarms per bed per day.
