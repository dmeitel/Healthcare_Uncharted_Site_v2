Rounds 04 · Problem Framing · Health AI

How Healthcare Gets Sold Its Problems

The Problem and the Product
TL;DR

Before healthcare buys a cure, somebody sells it the disease. The AI era runs the same trick one step downstream.

  • To sell OxyContin, the obstacle was clinicians' fear of addiction, so the fear got a diagnosis. "Pseudoaddiction" appeared in 224 published articles between 1989 and 2014, and no study ever validated it empirically. Purdue pleaded guilty to federal felonies twice, in 2007 and 2020, roughly $8.9 billion in federal resolutions.
  • The playbook is old and reusable. Restless legs prevalence was inflated from an honest 2.7% to "1 in 10 adults." "Low T" turned normal aging into a condition with a quiz and a $100 million ad budget, until the FDA said in 2015 the products were never approved for aging at all.
  • The AI version mostly skips inventing the disease and manufactures certainty about the cure instead. A proprietary sepsis model sold on a claimed AUC of 0.76 to 0.83 measured 0.63 in its first independent validation, missing two-thirds of sepsis cases while firing 109 alerts per true catch. Ambient scribes are the live case: real burnout relief, record adoption, and no demonstrated financial return yet.

Briefing

The certainty business

In 1969, a Brown & Williamson executive wrote the most honest sentence in the history of marketing: "Doubt is our product." The tobacco industry knew smoking killed people. Its job was to keep you from being sure. Naomi Oreskes and Erik Conway built a whole book around that machine, Merchants of Doubt, and the machine's one trick: when the evidence is against you, sell uncertainty about a real problem (Oreskes & Conway, 2010).

Healthcare has spent a century running the same machine in reverse. Doubt kills a market. Certainty builds one. If your product needs a problem and the honest problem is too small, you inflate it: fund an awareness campaign, commission a prevalence survey with generous criteria, and put a screening quiz where the ad used to be. The research literature named this in 2002: disease mongering, "widening the boundaries of treatable illness in order to expand markets for those who sell and deliver treatments" (Moynihan et al., 2002). Psychology named the underlying lever in 1981, when Tversky and Kahneman showed that the same facts, framed two ways, flip the majority decision (Tversky & Kahneman, 1981). Their founding experiment was, of all things, a disease outbreak.

So, three eras of the same move: the invented problem, the inflated problem, and the current version, where the problem is usually real and the thing being manufactured is certainty about the solution. I added this pattern to the site's Laws & Paradoxes field guide as the Framing Effect, because once you see it, you see it in every pitch deck that crosses your desk.

Assessment

Three readings of the same machine

Reading 1 · The invented problem · Greene & Chambers, 2015; Van Zee, 2009

To sell OxyContin at scale, Purdue needed clinicians to believe two things: that America had a massive undertreated-pain crisis, and that their fear of addiction was the obstacle standing in its way. The crisis got institutional muscle, "pain as the fifth vital sign" launched in 1996 and TJC pain standards followed in 2000, a mandated measurement with no instrument attached except the prescription pad (Baker, 2017). The fear got dissolved by a purpose-built concept. "Pseudoaddiction," coined in 1989 by an author who later became a Purdue executive, taught clinicians that drug-seeking behavior was actually a sign of undertreated pain, to be answered with MORE opioid (Van Zee, 2009). When researchers finally audited the concept in 2015, they found it in 224 published articles, and not one had ever tested it empirically. Every paper acknowledging industry funding was a proponent (Greene & Chambers, 2015). On that frame, OxyContin prescriptions went from about 300,000 in 1996 to nearly 6 million in 2001. The legal consequences came later: a felony misbranding plea and $634.5 million in 2007, three more felonies and an $8.3 billion resolution in 2020, a further $7.4 billion settlement with the Sacklers in 2025, and the APS, the professional society that carried the fifth vital sign, dissolved in 2019 amid opioid litigation (DOJ, 2007; DOJ, 2020; NY AG, 2025). Every count in those pleas traces back to how the problem was framed.

Reading 2 · The inflated problem · Woloshin & Schwartz, 2006; Layton et al., 2017; FDA, 2015

The softer version leaves the condition real and inflates the count. Restless legs syndrome is real neurology, but in 2003, two years before its drug was even approved, GSK announced that "1 in 10" adults was affected. The survey behind the number counted anyone who ever had symptoms; the share with symptoms frequent and severe enough to plausibly need a drug was 2.7 percent. Researchers then scored the news coverage: about two-thirds of articles repeated the inflated number, exactly one quantified the drug's actual benefit, and none mentioned overdiagnosis (Woloshin & Schwartz, 2006). "Low T" ran the same template at consumer scale. Normal age-related decline plus ordinary midlife tiredness got a name, a self-diagnosis quiz, and an ad budget that jumped from $14 million to $100 million in a single year. Testosterone use among men over 40 roughly tripled between 2001 and 2011, and a JAMA study matched DTC ad exposure to new prescriptions market by market, including hundreds of thousands of treatment starts, many with no baseline lab test at all (Layton et al., 2017). In 2015 the FDA said it outright in a safety communication: the products were never approved for age-related decline, and the label now had to warn about heart attack and stroke (FDA, 2015). After the TRAVERSE trial the FDA removed that cardiovascular warning in 2025, which softens the safety story and does nothing for the condition-invention one: the indication was never aging, and the market was built as if it were.

Reading 3 · The manufactured certainty · Wong et al., 2021; PHTI, 2025

The AI era mostly runs the machine one step downstream. The problems are real, sepsis kills, documentation burden is crushing clinicians, and what gets manufactured is certainty that the product solves them. Hundreds of hospitals switched on Epic's proprietary sepsis model largely on the vendor's reported performance, an AUC of 0.76 to 0.83. The first independent external validation, 27,697 patients at Michigan Medicine, measured 0.63. At its operating threshold the model caught 33 percent of sepsis cases, carried a 12 percent PPV, and made clinicians work through 109 alerts to find one true catch (Wong et al., 2021). Epic overhauled the model in 2022, after the paper, not before the sales (Ross, 2022). IBM Watson for Oncology was marketed worldwide as a cancer revolution while trained on synthetic cases reflecting a handful of specialists' preferences; internal documents later showed "unsafe and incorrect" recommendations, and IBM sold Watson Health off in pieces (Ross & Swetlitz, 2017; Ross, 2018). UnitedHealth's nH Predict ran the same manufactured certainty against patients, an unpublished algorithm projecting rehab stays for MA members while case managers were pressured to keep real stays within 1 to 3 percent of the projection (Ross & Herman, 2023). And ambient AI scribes are the live case: real burnout relief in the independent PHTI evaluation, the fastest adoption curve health IT has ever produced, nearly two-thirds of Epic hospitals by mid-2025, and no demonstrated financial ROI (PHTI, 2025). I cannot tell yet which way that one breaks; real relief has kept plenty of unprofitable technology installed before. The scribes are not fraud; they are adoption running ahead of evidence, with the vendor's problem slide doing the selling.

At the Bedside

The first slide is the sale

Every vendor deck I have ever sat through opens the same way. A crisis slide. Clinicians drowning, dollars leaking, and one big number in the corner of the slide. By the time slide two appears, the argument is over, because whoever writes the problem statement has already picked the solution. That is the Framing Effect doing its quiet work, and it is why the problem slide deserves more scrutiny than the demo does.

I came up in respiratory therapy while pain-scale charting was hardening into ritual. Every assessment, every patient, a mandated number for a subjective experience, because a marketing campaign had successfully reframed the absence of that number as a crisis of undertreatment. Clinicians inherited that frame, charted inside it, and got measured against it. The lesson I took into informatics is blunt: from the inside, a manufactured frame feels like policy.

So run the test that costs nothing. Take the crisis slide and hand it to your own clinicians and your own data team, and ask them to rewrite the problem from scratch, using your patients, your denominators, your baseline. A real crisis survives being rewritten by someone with nothing to sell; a manufactured one usually dies in the first meeting. Hospitals learned, expensively, to demand external validation of an algorithm. Almost nobody runs that same check on the problem statement, and the problem statement is where the money moves.

Follow-Up

Four things worth tracking

  1. Whether scribe ROI claims ever meet scribe ROI evidence. PHTI found real burnout relief and no demonstrated financial return. Watch whether the pitch quietly migrates from "time back for clinicians" to "revenue capture," because that migration is the tell that the problem statement was always negotiable (PHTI, 2025).
  2. Low T 2.0. The cardiovascular boxed warning came off in 2025 after TRAVERSE, and men's-health telehealth is already rebuilding the DTC testosterone funnel, quiz first. The condition-invention critique never depended on the safety signal. Watch whether the "awareness" spend returns to its 2012 shape.
  3. External validation as policy, not heroics. The Epic sepsis correction happened because one academic center chose to check. Watch whether regulators or purchasers ever make independent validation a condition of deployment rather than a courtesy, and whether vendors' claimed performance converges toward measured performance when they do.
  4. Algorithmic problem statements in coverage decisions. The nH Predict litigation and the Senate's MA scrutiny will decide whether an unpublished model's projection can keep functioning as the "objective" definition of how much care a patient needs. That is problem framing with a denial letter attached (Ross & Herman, 2023).

Leading indicators worth tracking

  • Vendor-funded prevalence surveys appearing ahead of product launches, the RLS pattern, in any new category
  • Whether health systems stand up problem-statement review alongside model governance in their AI committees
  • Independent external validations of deployed clinical AI, and the gap between claimed and measured performance in each one
  • DTC telehealth ad spend on testosterone and other lifestyle-adjacent conditions, quiz-first funnels especially
  • Follow-on PHTI-style evaluations measuring scribes on total cost and clinician outcomes rather than adoption
  • Court rulings on algorithm-driven MA denials, and any requirement that coverage algorithms be published or validated
Wider View

Keeping the two mechanisms straight

Be precise about what this argument is, because precision is what separates it from cynicism. There are two different moves documented here. Fabricating or inflating the PROBLEM: pseudoaddiction, halitosis, "1 in 10" restless legs, Low T. And manufacturing certainty about the SOLUTION to a problem that is real: a sepsis model's brochure numbers, Watson's synthetic training, an ROI slide with no ROI behind it. Collapsing the two into "healthcare is a scam" is how this critique gets dismissed. The unifying thread is narrower and better documented: someone with a product monetized the gap between what the evidence showed and what the audience was made to believe. In the tobacco era the gap was doubt; in the AI era it is mostly certainty, and either way it has been worth money.

And none of it requires a villain. South Korea produced a fifteen-fold thyroid-cancer "epidemic" with flat mortality out of nothing but cheap ultrasounds and fee-for-service incentives (Ahn et al., 2014). "Prediabetes" started life as an advocacy catchphrase and now covers a third of American adults (Piller, 2019). Incentives plus a detection apparatus will inflate a problem all by themselves. Which is exactly why the frame deserves institutional defenses (denominators, external validation, a rewrite test) instead of relying on every clinician to be individually immune to good marketing. They are not. Neither am I.

The problem statement is part of the product. Who gets paid to check it? Right now the answer is nobody, and I do not see who it is going to be.
