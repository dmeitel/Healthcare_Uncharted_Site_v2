The Bedside Task List Only Grows

Started With a Joke and Built a Model
TL;DR

I built a jokey little simulation of clinical task load and alarm fatigue. Not one of its thirty-two upgrades removes work, and neither does the floor.

  • The simulation has thirty-two things you can buy. Every one of them adds tasks. Not one subtracts. I wrote all of them and never saw it, because a mechanic for removal never occurred to me.
  • The real counts run the same direction. A month of monitoring in five ICUs logged 2,558,760 alarms, an audible burden of 187 per bed per day, and somewhere between 85% and 99% of alarm signals need no clinical intervention at all.
  • AI is arriving on the same terms. Predictive models went from 66% to 71% of US hospitals in a single year, and 44% of the hospitals running them had checked one for bias against their own patients.

Briefing

What ended up in the simulation

I spent about a week building a simulation of clinical task load, workload accumulation, and alarm fatigue. It runs like an idle clicker, the genre where you tap a button, buy something that taps for you, and watch as those additions make your work easier to do.

But the clicking wasn’t really the point. I was trying to visualize the pile-up: how many things a bedside worker is carrying at any given moment, how those demands accumulate, and what that accumulation looks like over the course of a long shift.

Near the end, I opened the upgrade table to sanity-check the pricing and realized just how much I’d managed to put into the simulation.

There were thirty-two purchasable things.

Pulse oximeters. Call lights. IV pumps. A telemetry bank. The EHR inbox. Overhead paging. A Joint Commission survey. A pizza party.

Somehow, I’d started with a joke about alarm fatigue and ended up building a crude model of the entire clinical environment.

Then I noticed the other thing.

Not one of those thirty-two upgrades removes work.

I wrote every one of them and never saw it. There is no button anywhere in that simulation that takes a task off you. It never occurred to me to build one, and the reason it never occurred to me is that in the years I spent running vents, and in the years since on the informatics side, I have never once sat in the meeting where that happens.

Concede the obvious first. Most of these additions were good ideas and plenty of them work. Barcode scanning catches wrong-patient and wrong-drug errors that used to reach people, and smart pumps stop overdoses that used to kill. Continuous pulse oximetry is the whole reason a desaturating patient gets found at 0300 rather than at shift change.

I would not pull any of that off a floor.

The problem is not that the additions were wrong. It is that nobody anywhere is doing the subtraction.

The Measures

How the work gets counted

Before any of the numbers below mean anything, it is worth knowing what a hospital actually counts when it counts nursing. There is a whole vocabulary for this and most people outside the building have never heard it.

The working vocabulary of nurse staffing
TermWhat it means
Nursing Hours per Patient Day (NHPPD)Nursing labor hours divided by patient days. The headline staffing number on most units.
Nurse staffing hoursHow much nursing labor was available and provided, before anyone asks what it was spent on.
Patient acuityHow sick and how complicated the patient is.
Patient classification system (PCS)The tool that turns acuity into a number, so a unit can argue for staffing with it.
Nursing intensityHow much nursing care a particular patient actually requires, and of what kind.
WorkloadHow much work is being asked of the staff on that shift.
Direct careHands on the patient.
Indirect careEverything else the patient needs done. Documentation, communication, coordination.
Nurse-sensitive indicatorsThe outcomes thought to move with nursing care. Falls, pressure injuries, certain infections.

So, that is the instrument panel. Now the uncomfortable part, which is that most of these gauges are softer than the decisions being made off them.

NHPPD is an average, and it is usually built on a midnight census. Count the patients at midnight, divide the hours by that, and you have your number. What the number cannot see is churn. Three admissions, two discharges and a transfer all happening between 1400 and 1800 is an enormous amount of nursing work that leaves no trace in a midnight count, because none of those people were in a bed when the count ran. An evaluation of NHPPD as a staffing measure concluded that it still needs further reliability testing across unit types and a consistent data collection procedure, and that its predictive validity varies by which outcome you check it against, holding up well for falls and poorly for pressure injuries (Min & Scott, 2016).

The acuity tools are worse off. Bonnie Jennings, writing the acuity chapter of AHRQ's handbook for nurses, set the problems with patient classification systems out plainly. Their validity and reliability, she wrote, are “infrequently monitored.” They “are not designed to detect census variability throughout the day from patient movement due to admissions, discharges, transfers, and short-stays,” which is the churn problem again in different clothes. She also found they “lack credibility among staff nurses and administrators,” something anyone who has watched a charge nurse fill one out at 0200 could have told her. And then the line that has stayed with me since I read it: their “focus on tasks shortchanges the cognitive work and knowledge inherent to expert nursing care and sophisticated surveillance” (Jennings, 2008). Watching a patient for the thing that has not happened yet is most of the job, and it scores as nothing.

Which leaves a gap between what gets counted and what happened, and there is a name for what falls into it. When Jane Ball and colleagues asked nurses what they had not managed to finish, 74% reported leaving some care undone on their last shift, and the odds of that roughly halved on shifts where a nurse had six patients or fewer instead of ten or more (Ball et al., 2016). None of that shows up in NHPPD. The hours were staffed and the hours were worked. The care was still not done.

I want to be careful here, because it would be easy to read this as the measures being useless. They are not. Aiken's staffing work rests on exactly these numbers, and the ratio laws that a dozen states have argued over are written in them. The honest version is narrower: the instruments are good enough to show that staffing matters and too blunt to show what a shift is actually made of. Everything after this section should be read with that in mind.

Assessment

What the additions add up to

Reading 1 · The alarm count · Drew et al., 2014; TJC, 2013

Start with the alarms, since that is what the game is named after. In 2014 a team at UCSF instrumented the physiologic monitors across five adult ICUs and counted everything for a month. Four hundred sixty-one consecutive patients, 2,558,760 alarms in thirty-one days. Of those, 381,560 were audible, which works out to an audible burden of 187 per bed per day (Drew et al., 2014). A nurse covering four beds is standing inside roughly 750 noises every twenty-four hours. Now the part that turns it from a nuisance into a safety problem. The Joint Commission put the share of alarm signals requiring no clinical intervention at somewhere between 85% and 99% (TJC, 2013). That range is enormous and I want to be honest that it bothers me; it is a thirteen year old figure with a fourteen point spread, it is quoted in nearly every paper on this subject, and I have not found a better national number to replace it with. Take the most conservative end and six of every seven alarms are still noise. The same alert counted 98 alarm-related events in its database over about three and a half years. Eighty of them ended with a dead patient.

Reading 2 · Where the shift actually goes · Bakhoum et al., 2021; Moy et al., 2021

The alarms are the audible part. The rest of what got added is quiet. A time and motion study at Vanderbilt put trained observers one on one with emergency nurses and clocked the split: 27% of the shift in the electronic record, 25% in direct patient care, 15% in indirect care (Bakhoum et al., 2021). The order matters more than the percentages. The record is the single most frequent task an emergency nurse performs. It beat the patients. And this work does not arrive in blocks you can settle into. A separate time and motion analysis measured how often clinicians switch tasks at all and landed on 1.4 switches per minute, with viewing or entering data involved in 48.2% of every switch (Moy et al., 2021). That is a task change about every forty-three seconds, all shift. Nobody sat down and designed a job that way. It accumulated on top of one that already existed.

Reading 3 · What AI is adding · Wong et al., 2021; Nong et al., 2025; ASTP, 2025

So here comes the next layer, and it is arriving fast. Predictive AI went from 66% of US hospitals in 2023 to 71% in 2024, and the growth is concentrated inside the EHR the nurse is already living in (ASTP, 2025). The pitch attached to these tools is always that the model will take work off the clinician. Look at what one actually delivered. When Michigan Medicine validated the Epic Sepsis Model externally, on their own patients, its discrimination fell to an AUC of 0.63. It missed 67% of the sepsis it existed to catch while firing alerts on 18% of every hospitalized patient in the building (Wong et al., 2021). Epic rebuilt the model and the revised version validates considerably better, which I went through in the AI module. But for the whole stretch it was switched on, that tool was a machine for manufacturing interruptions. And the checking underneath all of this is thinner than the adoption curve suggests. Of the hospitals using predictive models, 61% had validated one for accuracy against their own data and 44% had checked one for bias (Nong et al., 2025). More than half of these deployments have never been tested against the population they are firing at.

At the Bedside

There is no governance path for removal

Every hospital I have worked in has a governance path for adding a thing. There is a form. There is a committee that reviews the form. There is a build queue, a validation environment, a training module, a go-live date, and a name on a project plan. I have sat on that side of the table and helped run it. When somebody proposes a new alert, everyone in the room can tell you exactly which meeting approves it.

Ask the question the other direction and the room goes quiet. Which committee retires an alert? Who owns the list of things this unit no longer has to do? What is the form for removing a required field from an admission assessment?

I do not have an answer and I have been looking for a while. The closest thing I have seen up close is an alert tuning project, usually kicked off after a safety event, usually staffed by two people who have other jobs, usually finished once and never repeated. Addition has a standing process with a budget attached. Removal, when it happens at all, is somebody's side project. Nobody had to act in bad faith for that to be true, which is exactly why it is hard to fix.

And the load lands unevenly. The burnout data shows physicians climbing out of the 2021 peak four years running while nurse turnover turned back up in 2025. The additions landed on both groups. The nurses are the ones still walking out the door. Linda Aiken put a number on the mechanism more than two decades ago: each extra patient added to a nurse's load came with a 7% jump in patient mortality and a 23% jump in the odds of that nurse burning out (Aiken et al., 2002). An alarm is a small version of that same extra patient. So is a scan, and so is one more required field on a form.

Wider View

Why an idle clicker was the right form

I picked the genre because idle clickers are funny and I wanted to build something people would actually open and click on. That it also fit the argument showed up afterward, and it took me by surprise.

What I actually wanted out of it was simultaneity. Four alarms, a call light, a relative at the desk and a chat message all landing inside the same ten seconds, which is the part that never survives a description of the job. Written down as a list of duties it sounds ordinary. An idle clicker can do the pile-up, because the genre is built so that everything runs at once and keeps getting faster.

The rule of the genre is that the number only goes up. That is the entire contract between the game and the player. You buy a thing, your rate increases, you buy a bigger thing, it increases more, and the game never once asks you to hand anything back. Players find that deeply satisfying. It is also the exact shape of the thing that is grinding down the people the game is about, and I did not engineer that in. The form arrived with it already installed.

There is one mechanic I did build on purpose and it is the one I would defend in a meeting. The button that quiets the floor is labeled Silence, not Mute, because Silence is what the button on a real monitor says and it is what that button actually does. It buys you a moment. Then anything new you buy clears it, because the new device does not know you pressed it. If you want the floor to go quiet and stay that way, you have to open the mixer and take eleven separate alarm families down to zero, one at a time. There is no master volume in there. I took it out.

Now the honest limit. This is a browser game. It has no control group, it measures nothing, and every number in it was tuned by me over a weekend until it felt about right. It proves nothing. I built it to be played. The argument is in the sources underneath it.

Follow-Up

What would show this changing

Signals worth tracking

  • Any health system that publishes a retirement rate for clinical alerts alongside its deployment count, the way it publishes uptime
  • Whether AI governance committees are given authority to remove an existing alert as a condition of approving a new one
  • A replacement for the 2013 alarm figure, meaning a current national measure of what share of alarm signals are actionable
  • Nurse-specific time and motion work repeated post-ambient-AI, measuring total task count rather than documentation minutes alone
  • Local validation rates for predictive models, especially the bias number, and whether the gap to adoption narrows or widens
  • Whether the required fields on an admission assessment at any given hospital go down in a year, ever, by any amount
If you play it, you will reach 1800 with a task list longer than the one you clocked in with. That was not a design decision. I just could not work out what the button that shortens it would say.
